Most of us learned sex on autopilot. The same touch, the same pace, the same destination —
patterns laid down years ago and never questioned. Somewhere along the way, we stopped
listening.

This work is not therapy, and it's not performance. I'm a somatic sex educator: I don't fix
people, because you aren't broken. My work is helping you build connection and awareness with
your own body and its pleasures. Together, we create a structured, consent-based container
where you can slow down, pay attention, and let your body teach you what it actually wants.
Not what porn suggested. Not what a partner expected. Not what habit delivers. What *you*
want, discovered in real time, in your own skin.

## What is Erotic Embodiment?

Erotic Embodiment grew out of thirty years of quiet, dedicated research — a network of
practitioners exploring erotic massage and mindful self-touch, paying close attention not to
technique but to *inner experience*. What they found was remarkable: when we bring full
presence to arousal, the erotic state becomes a doorway. On the other side is wisdom,
creativity, aliveness, and a freedom that ordinary consciousness rarely touches.

You may have encountered this work under its earlier name, Orgasmic Yoga. The word "orgasmic"
works the way "fitness" works in "fitness center" — it names the territory, not the entry
requirement. You don't have to feel orgasmic to practice. Some days the practice feels clunky,
distracted, emotional. That's not failure; that's the practice. We show up, we witness what
arises with compassion, and over time we become more embodied, more attuned, more free.

<h2 id="realms">Feel. Connect. Play.</h2>

Sex researcher Donald Mosher observed that people experience sex in three distinct realms:
sex to **feel** (trance — pure sensation and inner experience), sex to **connect** (partner
engagement — eyes, affection, mutuality), and sex to **play** (role and fantasy — scripts and
scenes).

Most of us live in one realm and feel awkward, or lost, in the others. Sexual wellbeing is
fluency in all three.

Ask yourself: which realm is home for you? Which have you never truly visited? This work's
specialty is the least-taught realm of all — the deep trance of feeling — and from that
foundation, the other two open more easily.

## The body epiphany

Somatic pioneer Ed Maupin believed embodiment begins with a "body epiphany" — a moment when
you suddenly notice the aliveness you carry inside. Teachers since have found these openings
don't have to be rare lightning strikes; within a mindfulness practice, they can become
regular visitors.

That's the heart of what I facilitate: creating the conditions for your body to open, and
then — just as important — protecting the time to savor and integrate what arrives. The
opening is not the learning. The absorption is.

## How the body learns

Thomas Hanna defined *soma* as "the body experienced from within" — and the body learns
differently than the mind. You won't learn new ways of being erotic by watching or reading,
any more than you'd learn piano by watching concerts. The body learns through conscious
practice over time, self-directed but well supported, ideally in the company of at least one
other person.

And it learns through difficulty. Deep practice feels like exploring a dark room: you move
slowly, bump into things, pause, try again — until one day you move through the space with
ease. The fumbling isn't a detour from the learning. It *is* the learning.

## Mindfulness meets sexuality

At its core, this work rests on a simple equation: **somatic sex education = sex education +
mindfulness.**

Neuroscience has confirmed what contemplative traditions have long known — sustained
mindfulness practice literally reshapes the brain. Researchers like Daniel Siegel have shown
that mindful repetition strengthens the very capacities that make a life work: emotional
balance, body regulation, insight, empathy, the ability to pause before acting.

Erotic Embodiment applies this science to the one domain mindfulness traditions mostly left
out: our sexuality. Conscious repetition is how humans learn *anything* — including sex. Most
of us simply repeated our way into habits we never chose. Through practice, we can repeat our
way into something better.

## Activation and installation

Here's a piece of this work I find genuinely revolutionary. Psychologist Rick Hanson's
research shows that growth is a two-stage process: we *activate* an experience, and then — if
we want it to change us — we must *install* it. Our brains evolved to encode threat
automatically and let pleasure slide right off. A beautiful experience, left unabsorbed,
simply evaporates.

So in this work, we don't chase peak states and move on. Every practice session ends in
stillness — a few minutes of savoring, like savasana at the end of a yoga class — where
sensation is absorbed, neural pathways are honored, and the experience moves from a moment
into a capacity. Gratitude helps it land. Time helps it root. This is why the work *lasts*.

## What pleasure actually is

Here's a definition worth keeping: excitement is a gradual *rise* in the nervous system's
firing. Bliss is a gradual *quieting*. Pleasure is the interplay between the two — the dance
of building and releasing, arousal and enjoyment.

Most of us received not one hour of education in accessing or savoring either. Worse, many of
us were actively trained out of both — *sit still, don't touch, you shouldn't feel that way*.
The strategy children learn for avoiding shameful feelings is to place attention outside the
body, and the cost is total: we dim *all* sensation, not just the erotic. Distracting
ourselves from pleasure is one definition of shame.

This work is the education we never got. Not fixing what shame broke — simply, patiently,
teaching the body what it was always capable of feeling.

## The numbness nobody talks about

Do you remember who you've had good sex with — but not what it *felt* like? Do you check out
during sex, or need porn or fantasy just to get there? Has your body gone quiet from the
waist down?

Countless people carry an unconscious numbness in their genitals and pelvis — somatics
pioneer Thomas Hanna called it *sensory-motor amnesia*. It usually forms early: shame teaches
us to place our attention outside the body, chronic tension does the rest, and most of us
never even know our capacity for feeling has shrunk. The tense, hurried, silent self-touch
patterns learned at puberty become lifelong physiological scripts.

Here's the good news: numbness is not damage. It's a learned pattern — and what was learned
can be unlearned. Attention, breath, movement, and patient touch thaw what shame froze.
Sensation returns.

## Tolerating bliss

Here is the secret almost no one tells you: we don't only resist our pain. We resist our
pleasure.

When the body truly wakes up — when breath moves freely and sensation begins to shimmer — the
lightness can feel almost unbearable, and we reflexively contract back to what's familiar.
That bracing against our own aliveness is itself a source of the tension we complain about.

Learning to bear the deliciousness — to stay open one breath longer than the reflex allows —
may be the most important capacity this work builds. It changes not just how you experience
pleasure, but how much life you can let in.

## It begins with breath

If everything else about this work feels foreign, start here: you already know how to breathe.

Breath is the bridge — the one function of the body that runs unconsciously yet answers
instantly to conscious choice. With it, you can calm your whole system or awaken it. Deep
belly breathing physically engages the pelvic floor and stirs the genital nerves; the erotic
is built into your respiratory system, waiting.

You'll learn breath that relaxes, breath that arouses, and breath that circulates energy
through the whole body. These simple, learnable practices are remarkably effective for
deadened desire, overwhelming desire, erectile difficulties, early ejaculation, and difficulty
reaching orgasm — not as treatment, but as education. Skills, not cures.

## Why work with a coach?

Because self-directed practice is exactly where most people stall. Resistance, distraction,
boredom, "I'll do it tomorrow" — the practice that could change everything is the easiest
thing in the world to skip.

A coach changes that. In our work together I offer what the great Betty Dodson — who invented
sex coaching — defined as its craft: permission, encouragement, instruction, reframing, and
guidance for where you place your attention. Plus the simplest gift of all: someone who knows
you're practicing, and asks how it went.

## Practice is the teacher

I can guide, structure, demonstrate, and witness — but the deepest teacher in this work is
your own daily practice. Sessions of breath, touch, movement, sound, and attention, done in
the privacy of your own life, at your own pace, guided by your own intention. Thirty minutes
is a beautiful minimum, but presence matters far more than duration.

Even how we drift away from practice, and how we find our way back, becomes part of the
learning.

This is what I mean when I say the body is a source of wisdom. Not as a metaphor — as a
method. Your body already knows things your mind has been too busy to hear. This work is how
we sit down and listen.

## If you're thinking "this isn't for me"

Pay attention to that thought. *I don't have time for this. I don't see the point. I could
never do something like that.*

Here's a truth from decades of this work: many people — including sex educators themselves —
have lost the ability to arouse themselves through sensuous play with their own bodies. The
capacity froze somewhere along the way, and the freeze often disguises itself as busyness,
skepticism, or indifference. If the very idea of slowing down with your own body makes you
want to close this tab, that resistance isn't a sign this work is wrong for you. It's often
the clearest sign it's exactly what your nervous system has been waiting for.

This isn't a judgment. It's an invitation — and it's precisely what one-on-one guidance is
for. You don't have to thaw alone.

## A word about porn

If pornography or fantasy is currently how your arousal works, you will not be shamed for
that here. Porn makes arousal efficient and effortless — that's its whole design — and for
many people it's become the sole doorway in. The cost is that the doorway narrows over time,
and the body's own native eroticism goes quiet.

We don't start by taking the doorway away. We start where you are — using what already works
for you, if needed — and gently, progressively, teach your body to generate arousal from the
inside. From breath. From movement. From your own hands and attention. What most people
discover is that they don't have to give anything up; their erotic world simply gets bigger.

## What clients report

Not fireworks (though those happen). The changes people describe are quieter and bigger:
greater freedom. Greater aliveness. A sense of flow between parts of themselves that had gone
silent to each other. Deep relaxation paired, surprisingly, with alertness. And a
relationship with their own body that feels — often for the first time — like a friendship.
